## Summary
Connecticut has comprehensive legislation and training programs aimed at combating human trafficking, particularly focusing on sex trafficking. The state requires annual basic human trafficking training for all Child Protective Services staff and recommends advanced training for those who choose. Various training sessions are designed for DCF staff, foster parents, schools, law enforcement, hospitals, hospitality industry professionals, and youth.

## Extracted Requirements
- **Applicable industry:** Child protective services, foster care, schools, law enforcement, hospitals, hotels/motels/lodging, medical providers, emergency services, and youth programs.
- **Training required:** Annual basic human trafficking training for DCF staff. Advanced training is recommended.
- **Signage required:** Not explicitly mentioned in the source text.
- **Reporting required:** State attorneys and local law enforcement must document training, investigations of missing children, trafficking referrals, trafficking cases referred for prosecution, etc.
- **Enforcement agency:** Department of Children and Families (DCF), law enforcement (state and municipal).
- **Legislation:** Multiple public acts addressing human trafficking, including sex trafficking. Specific laws include 2017 Public Act 17-32, 2016 Public Act 16-71, 2015 Public Act 15-195, 2014 Public Act 14-186, and 2013 Public Act 13-166.
- **Mandatory data reporting:** Yes, according to multiple public acts.

## Analysis
Connecticut's approach to combating human trafficking is robust, incorporating both legislative measures and training programs. The requirement for annual basic training ensures that front-line workers are equipped with the knowledge necessary to identify and respond to trafficking situations effectively. Additionally, the state's legislation covers various aspects of trafficking, including age-related offenses, hotel/motel requirements, victim services, and crime prevention.

While there is no explicit mention of signage requirements in the provided sources, it is common practice for states to require signage in high-traffic areas where human trafficking may occur. This could be an area for future implementation based on observed needs and best practices.
